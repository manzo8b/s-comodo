*You can book a table in seconds. You cannot book the feeling of belonging.*

## The table nobody reserved

Imagine an ordinary morning in an Italian neighbourhood café. Someone walks in, and the person behind the counter already knows what to prepare. A customer who has just moved to the area waits beside the counter. A remark about the weather becomes a conversation. No one reserved a table. No one arranged a meeting. And yet, for a few minutes, strangers share the same small world.

Now imagine a gathering in Paraguay. A thermos of cold water is nearby. There is yerba mate, a *guampa* and a *bombilla*. In the traditions surrounding *tereré*, preparing and sharing a drink can become a reason to slow down and spend time together. The conversation belongs to the people, not to the objects.

These are illustrative scenes, not reports from a particular café or Paraguayan household. Their histories and customs are different. But each scene brings us towards a question that is becoming unexpectedly contemporary: **what turns an ordinary meeting into the feeling that we belong somewhere?**

An October 2026 survey from the Pew Research Center offers a revealing place to begin. Nearly four in ten American adults say they are part of an online community. Among those aged 18 to 29, the share is 48%. Some find connection, information and support in these spaces that would be difficult to obtain elsewhere. But being part of a community and feeling close to its members are not identical experiences. [1](#fonte-1)

Perhaps our question should not be whether the internet has replaced the places where people meet. **Perhaps it is what makes us want to return — and what makes someone notice when we do not.**

## Connected, but how close?

The Pew-Knight Initiative surveyed 9,750 adults in the United States between 26 May and 1 June 2026. Its nationally representative findings describe US adults, not humanity as a whole; they record reported experiences and opinions, not a causal explanation of loneliness. [2](#fonte-2)

Among Americans who belong to online communities, roughly seven in ten say these spaces add value to their lives. About six in ten say their most-visited community offers something they could only find online. These are not small benefits, especially for people separated by distance or united by interests that may be rare where they live. [1](#fonte-1)

At the same time, the feelings of closeness are mixed. About one in five online-community members say they feel *extremely* or *very* close to others in the space they visit most; another 37% feel *somewhat* close. Nearly half say they do not feel very close, including 14% who do not feel close at all. The survey also finds that 68% knew very few people or no one in that community before joining. [1](#fonte-1)

The figures invite a more subtle reading than “the internet makes us lonely”. A digital group can be useful without being intimate. It can also become a source of friendship, particularly when geography makes an in-person community difficult to find. Some online groups arrange physical gatherings too. We should not confuse **the number of connections**, **the value of a community** and **the depth of a relationship**.

There is an older vocabulary for thinking about this difference.

## The invention of the third place

In his 1989 book *The Great Good Place*, the American sociologist Ray Oldenburg popularised the idea of the **third place**: neither the home, with its domestic relationships, nor work, with its obligations and roles, but an informal setting where people can meet regularly. Years later, Oldenburg and Karen Christensen revisited the concept in *The UNESCO Courier*. [3](#fonte-3)

Third places can be cafés, neighbourhood bars, shops and community spaces. The important thing is not the label or the furniture. It is the possibility of entering without a special invitation, talking without a formal agenda and becoming familiar through repeated encounters. Oldenburg's account emphasises accessibility, low cost, conversation and the presence of regulars who do not automatically make strangers feel unwelcome. [3](#fonte-3)

The phrase has since become commercially attractive. It can appear in descriptions of beautiful cafés with expensive menus, limited seating or a quiet expectation that customers should not stay too long. A carefully designed interior may be pleasant. **It does not become a community simply because someone calls it one.**

The real question is who can enter, who can afford to stay, and who is made to feel that their presence is welcome.

## London's penny universities — and their closed doors

The modern café did not invent the social importance of coffee. Historian Markman Ellis traces London's seventeenth-century coffeehouses, beginning in 1652, to models already established in Ottoman territories. In London, they developed into places where printed news, commerce, conversation and political argument met. [5](#fonte-5)

By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, some London coffeehouses were known as *penny universities*: for a modest payment, customers could find coffee, papers and discussion. The Courtauld Institute's historical account describes writers, merchants, tradespeople and travellers meeting in rooms where information circulated almost as quickly as gossip. These businesses were predecessors of neither the modern newsroom nor the university in any strict institutional sense; the nickname captured the experience of informal learning. [4](#fonte-4)

There is a temptation to portray those rooms as democratic utopias. The history is less comfortable. Ellis shows that the apparent freedom of coffeehouse conversation was regulated by tacit expectations, and that women were frequently excluded from the very debates later celebrated as open. [5](#fonte-5)

Here is the difficulty hidden inside nostalgia: **a place can create belonging for some people by making other people feel they do not belong.** The quality of a gathering place cannot be measured only by the affection of its regulars. It must also be measured by the doors it leaves open.

## A guampa passed around

In Paraguay, *tereré* begins from a different history. It is not an Italian espresso served cold, nor a picturesque prop in a story about modern cafés. It is a cultural practice rooted in Paraguayan and Guaraní knowledge, with its own preparation, vocabulary, objects and ways of sharing.

In 2020, UNESCO inscribed the practices and traditional knowledge associated with tereré within the culture of *pohã ñana* on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Its description connects the practice to family transmission, the use and knowledge of herbs, and occasions for inclusion, friendship, dialogue, respect and solidarity. [6](#fonte-6)

It would be a mistake to claim that everyone in Paraguay prepares or experiences tereré in the same way. And a ritual is not, strictly speaking, a physical *third place*: the gathering can happen in a home, at work or outdoors. Yet the distinction is illuminating. **Sometimes people create a sense of place around a repeated gesture, not the other way round.**

An Italian café has an address. A tereré gathering has a practice that can travel. What connects the two is not a claim of cultural equivalence but a question about time: when we share it with others, do we have to justify it as productive?

## The architecture of staying

Nostalgia is not much use if there is nowhere to sit.

In a study of commercial gathering places on main streets in two cities and one town in Massachusetts, Vikas Mehta and Jennifer K. Bosson found that recognisability and openness towards the street distinguished many sociable venues, while seating and shelter were particularly important to social interaction. Their study was local and observational: it identifies useful design features, not a universal recipe for friendship. [7](#fonte-7)

Still, it makes an important point. Social life depends on physical details. A library cannot be a welcoming refuge if its hours exclude those who need it. A public square may be legally open but difficult to use without shade, accessible seating or a safe way to arrive. A café can be generous to its neighbourhood while still needing to cover its rent.

The answer is not to ask every private business to provide endless free hospitality. Cities need a mix of commercial, civic and genuinely public spaces — places where spending money is not always the admission ticket to ordinary human company.

And the feeling of welcome matters as much as the architecture. Is there somewhere to pause? Can a newcomer understand how to join in? Do the regulars make room for someone new? If a person returns next week, will the place feel a little less anonymous?

## Not an argument against screens

The World Health Organization's 2025 Commission on Social Connection report describes loneliness and social isolation as significant concerns for health and society. It does not establish that any single technological development, or the closure of a particular kind of café, caused those problems. [8](#fonte-8)

Likewise, Pew's 2026 survey should not be made to say that digital relationships are inevitably superficial. Its findings include real benefits and meaningful closeness alongside weaker ties and doubts about online connection. These experiences can coexist. [1](#fonte-1)

For people living far from family, for those with limited mobility, or for someone whose interests are scarcely represented locally, a digital gathering can be invaluable. Online contact can also lead to an in-person meeting. The distinction between virtual and physical community is not always a wall.

The problem is not that technology provides another way to belong. It is that **we should have more than one way**.

## The place that remembers you

Return, for a moment, to the Italian café. The ordinary greeting matters not because a barista remembers a product, but because a person might feel noticed. Return to the Paraguayan gathering: the ritual matters not because an object guarantees friendship, but because it may create time and occasion for people to be together.

Neither picture is universal. Both can exclude. Both can be romanticised. A welcoming place has to earn that description through what happens there, not through what a writer wishes it to represent.

History does not give us a single model to copy. London's coffeehouses reveal the possibilities and boundaries of public conversation. Oldenburg supplies a language for informal meeting places. Research on urban design shows how practical a welcoming space must be. Tereré reminds us that belonging can be carried in a habit as well as built into a street. And the Pew survey shows that digital connection has its own richness and its own unresolved questions.

Maybe belonging is not the ability to reach thousands of people at once. Maybe, at times, it is something quieter: **a place or practice we can return to, and people who make room when we do.**

*The tables can be reserved. The feeling cannot.*
